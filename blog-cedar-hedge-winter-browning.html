Why cedars turn brown over winter — and how to stop it.

Every April we get the same photo from homeowners: a cedar hedge that went into winter green and came out rust-brown on one side. Most people assume the cold killed it. Usually, it didn't. Cedars rarely brown from cold alone. They brown from drying out — and a lot of that damage is set up in October and November, right after the sprinklers go off for the season.

Our team doesn't wrap or prune hedges. But we install drip zones on cedar hedges all over York Region, and this is what we tell every customer when we close their system in the fall.

Why do cedars turn brown over the winter?

Quick answer

Winter browning is mostly dehydration. Sun and wind pull moisture out of the foliage all winter, but frozen roots can't replace it. A dry fall, road salt spray and exposed, windy locations make it much worse. Cold on its own is rarely the cause.

The damage almost always shows on the side facing the road, the prevailing wind, or the winter sun. That pattern is the giveaway. If only the south or street side browned, it's winter burn, not disease.

Is it normal for cedars to go brown inside in the fall?

Quick answer

Yes. Every fall, cedars shed older foliage from the inside of the plant. It turns yellow-brown and drops from September into November. If the outer tips are still green, that's normal needle drop, not winter damage.

Don't panic and don't prune it out. Shaking the hedge gently or running a gloved hand through it clears the old foliage. The browning to worry about is on the outer surface, and it shows up in late winter and spring.

How much should I water cedars in the fall?

Quick answer

Give cedars a deep, slow soak about once a week when it hasn't rained, right up until the ground freezes — typically late November or December in York Region. Water the soil at the base, not the foliage. Moist soil going into winter is the best protection.

Here's the catch for sprinkler owners: once your system is blown out, your drip zone on the hedge is off too. The weeks between the fall closing and freeze-up are exactly when cedars need water most and get it least.

A few practical ways to handle it:

  • Lay a garden hose at the base on a slow trickle and move it along the hedge every 20 to 30 minutes.
  • Use a soaker hose along the base for the fall, then drain it and put it away before freezing.
  • Do this before you shut off and drain your outdoor taps for winter. Plan one last deep soak right before you close them.

Newly planted cedars — anything in its first two or three winters — are the priority. Their roots haven't spread far enough to find deeper moisture yet.

Should I wrap my cedars in burlap?

Quick answer

Burlap helps young cedars and hedges exposed to road salt or strong wind. Put it up as a screen on stakes along the exposed side rather than wrapping it tight against the foliage. A tight wrap traps moisture and can cause its own damage.

Burlap is worth it if your hedge:

  • Faces a road or driveway where plows throw salty slush
  • Sits in an open, windy spot with no shelter
  • Is in its first few winters

An established hedge in a sheltered backyard usually doesn't need it. Put screens up in late November, once it's consistently cold, and take them down in early spring.

How do I stop snow from splitting my cedars?

Quick answer

Tall, upright cedars can splay open under heavy, wet snow. Loosely spiral garden twine around them in late fall to hold the branches together, and remove it in spring. After a heavy snowfall, gently brush snow off with a broom, pushing upward.

Never shake or knock off ice. Frozen branches snap. Let ice melt on its own.

Should I mulch, fertilize or prune cedars in the fall?

Quick answer

Mulch yes, fertilizer and pruning no. A 5 to 8 cm layer of mulch holds soil moisture and evens out soil temperature. Keep it a few centimetres back from the trunks. Late-season fertilizer or pruning pushes tender growth that winter kills.

Save any shaping for late spring or early summer.

My cedars browned last winter. Are they dead?

Quick answer

Often not. Wait until late May or June before deciding. Scratch a small twig with your thumbnail — green underneath means it's alive. Cedars regrow slowly from brown areas, but a hedge that was well watered usually recovers over a season or two.

If the same hedge browns every winter, the root cause is usually water, not weather.

The long-term fix: a drip zone on the hedge

Spray heads were never designed for hedges. They wet the foliage, miss the root zone, and throw water on the driveway. A dedicated drip zone puts water right at the base of the hedge, which keeps cedars properly hydrated all season. That's what builds the reserves they need to get through winter.

We add drip zones to existing sprinkler systems without re-trenching the whole yard. Read how it works in Drip Irrigation for Established Gardens & Hedges, or see our drip irrigation service.

Plan a drip zone for the hedge

Fall is a good time to plan it. Just planted a hedge, or planning one for next year? Mention it to our technician at your fall closing, and they'll note the details on your work order. Book a look at your hedge online or call, and we can have it installed when systems start up in spring.
